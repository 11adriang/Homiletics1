Course Description

This course introduces students to the foundational skills of sermon preparation for preaching the Gospel. Students will learn the role of the Holy Spirit in preaching, effective communication techniques, and proper sermon outline development. Through a Pentecostal lens, the course emphasizes the divine mandate and supernatural empowerment for proclaiming God's Word, aiming to build confidence in crafting biblically sound, Spirit-empowered messages that lead to life transformation.

Course Objectives

  • Gain confidence in preparing biblically sound sermons with the help of the Holy Spirit.
  • Develop clear and organized sermon outlines.
  • Identify and apply key principles of effective communication in sermon preparation.
  • Demonstrate understanding of sermon structure and purpose in writing.
  • Use sound hermeneutics in the preparation of a sermon outline.

The Mandate to Preach

Topics Covered

  • 2 Timothy 4:2 — the preacher's charge
  • What preaching is: proclaiming and declaring the truth
  • Ministering to the heart, not the head
  • The three steps for preparing the material
  • The four steps for preparing yourself

Learning Objectives (ABCD method)

  1. By the end of the session, the student will recite 2 Timothy 4:2 from memory and explain in his or her own words what it means to be instant in season and out of season, with 100% accuracy.
  2. By the end of the session, the student will list from memory the three steps for preparing material — familiarize, categorize, organize — and the four steps for preparing oneself, without notes.
  3. By the end of the session, the student will explain in writing the difference between ministering to the heart and ministering to the head, giving at least two practical examples.
  4. By the end of the session, the student will state the three marks of good delivery and identify, given a sample sermon clip, which of the three is weakest, defending the answer in two or three sentences.

A: audience · B: observable behavior · C: condition · D: degree

I.

The Charge

2 Timothy 4:2KJV

Preach the word; be instant in season, out of season; reprove, rebuke, exhort with all longsuffering and doctrine.

A.

Preach the Word

  • Preaching is proclaiming and declaring the truth despite the circumstances.
  • Preaching is done from a conviction of what is real to you.
B.

Be Instant — Be Ready

The charge is not to be ready when it is convenient, but in season and out of season. Readiness is a settled condition, not an occasional mood.

Teacher’s Note

Ask the class: when was the last time you were asked to speak with no warning? What happened? Readiness is built long before the moment arrives.

C.

Minister to the Heart, Not the Head

Minister to the heart, not the head — and from the heart, not from the head. A message that only informs the mind has not yet done its work.

II.

Steps for Preparing

A.

Preparing the Material

  1. Familiarize — with all material.
  2. Categorize — in sub-topics.
  3. Organize — in the presentation order.
B.

Preparing Yourself

  1. Read yourself full (of the Word).
  2. Think yourself clear.
  3. Pray yourself hot.
  4. Preach yourself happy.

Teacher’s Note

These four are sequential. A preacher who prays hot without first reading full will be passionate and empty; one who reads full but never prays hot will be accurate and cold.

III.

Delivery

  1. Flow with the Spirit — don't be nervous or hasty.
  2. Don't distract your audience.
  3. Give your audience something to remember (that they can use).

Preaching at its best is a manifestation of the Living Word, developed from the Written Word, and delivered by the Spoken Word.

I.

Defining the Discipline

Homiletics

The science and art of the preparation and delivery of sermons. The art and science of preparing, proclaiming, and delivering the Word of God to His people.

Hermeneutics

The art and science of biblical interpretation.

Homilein

To communicate, to speak. Preaching is speaking and proclaiming the Word of God.

A.

The Four Terms in the Definition

  1. Science — it touches phonetics, acoustics, semantics, and psychology, as well as many other disciplines.
  2. Art — preaching has room for an infinite variety of styles and creative innovation, just as other arts do. There is no one right way to preach.
  3. Preparation.
  4. Delivery.
II.

The Pattern: Nehemiah 8:8

Nehemiah 8:8KJV

So they read in the book in the law of God distinctly, and gave the sense, and caused them to understand the reading.

  1. They read distinctly from the book.
    • This gives attention to clear, oral delivery.
  2. They gave the sense.
    • This gives attention to exegesis and exposition.
  3. They caused them to understand the reading.
    • This demonstrates a follow-through to a state of assimilation and self-application by the audience.
A.

The Preacher's Fundamental Task

The preacher's fundamental task is one of translation. He takes the words of Scripture and renders them alive and full of meaning to those who listen. The preacher is a transmission line and a lens to make clear what the Bible says. His work is to start at the Book and end at the people.

  • The preacher is an announcer, a proclaimer of the truth.
  • Preaching can be thought of as the sharing of strong convictions. The preacher must speak with a sure word and a positive spirit.
  • Preaching is also, in a very literal sense, speaking for God. In this meaning it performs a prophetic function.
  • Preaching, when it consists of a message that is truly from God to the people, is essentially indistinguishable from prophecy.
III.

The Call to Preach

You are the vessel that God is going to use to get His message to His people.

Romans 10:14KJV

How then shall they call on him in whom they have not believed? and how shall they believe in him of whom they have not heard? and how shall they hear without a preacher?

Teacher’s Note

YOU CANNOT SPEAK ON BEHALF OF GOD IF YOU HAVE NEVER ENCOUNTERED HIM. Walk the class through Exodus 2:23 – 3:14: Moses is not sent until he has met God at the bush.

A.

Discerning the Call

  • Discerning God's voice.
  • Internal conviction.
  • External confirmation.
Isaiah 6:8KJV

Also I heard the voice of the Lord, saying, Whom shall I send, and who will go for us? Then said I, Here am I; send me.

Acts 13:2-3KJV

As they ministered to the Lord, and fasted, the Holy Ghost said, Separate me Barnabas and Saul for the work whereunto I have called them. [3] And when they had fasted and prayed, and laid their hands on them, they sent them away.

Jeremiah 1:5-9KJV

Before I formed thee in the belly I knew thee; and before thou camest forth out of the womb I sanctified thee, and I ordained thee a prophet unto the nations. [6] Then said I, Ah, Lord GOD! behold, I cannot speak: for I am a child. [7] But the LORD said unto me, Say not, I am a child: for thou shalt go to all that I shall send thee, and whatsoever I command thee thou shalt speak. [8] Be not afraid of their faces: for I am with thee to deliver thee, saith the LORD. [9] Then the LORD put forth his hand, and touched my mouth. And the LORD said unto me, Behold, I have put my words in thy mouth.

Preaching is a sacred trust and an act of worship.

B.

Pentecostal Distinctives in Preaching

  • The Holy Spirit's anointing.
  • Spiritual gifts.
  • Divine encounter.
  • Life transformation.

  2. By the end of the session, the student will list the five listener tendencies and the prescribed response to each, matching all five correctly.
  3. By the end of the session, the student will explain in writing why a sermon must be interesting before it can be clear, convincing, or action-producing.
  4. By the end of the session, the student will analyze John 4:1-24 and identify where Jesus performs each of the four moves of good communication, citing the verse for each.

A: audience · B: observable behavior · C: condition · D: degree

I.

God's Communication Principles

  1. The first principle of God's communication is Divine Revelation.
    • God communicates with man to give us knowledge and guidance far beyond what we could ever find for ourselves.
  2. The next principle is Mediation.
    • Human instruments convey His revelation to bridge the communication gap that stands between Himself and man.
  3. Finally, the principle of Illumination.
    • This allows the Word to become personal to the listener.

Teacher’s Note

Point out that the preacher lives in the middle principle. Revelation is God's work and illumination is the Spirit's work; mediation is the part entrusted to us.

II.

Preaching and Teaching

  • Both preaching and teaching should convey truth and be anointed and inspiring.
  • If you are serious about preaching, you must also be willing to teach. Most aspiring preachers entering the ministry will have opportunities to teach sooner and much more frequently than preaching opportunities.
  • A preacher should know everything any good public speaker knows — and more.
III.

Executing Good Communication

A.

The Four Moves

  • Capture the listener's attention.
  • Connect with the listener's interests.
  • Stimulate the listener's desire.
  • Invite the listener's action or participation.

Teacher’s Note

Jesus did all four of these with the woman at the well in John 4:1-24. Walk the passage slowly and let the class locate each move.

B.

Assume These Five Tendencies in Every Listener

  1. Boredom
    • So, start a fire and ignite their interest.
  2. Self-Interest
    • So, build a bridge so as to connect with the listener.
  3. Lack of Imagination
    • So, open many windows by using vivid illustrations.
  4. Weak Memory
    • Offer a basket to hold their thoughts in, by using lists, memorable outlines, acronyms, and the like.
  5. Uncertainty of Action
    • Plant a sign post by showing the person the next step to take after they have heard your message.
C.

All Sermons Should Be

  • Interesting.
  • Clear — but it must be interesting too.
  • Convincing — but it must be interesting and clear first.
  • Producing action — but it must first be interesting, clear, and convincing.

The order is cumulative, not optional. Each quality rests on the one before it.

WEEK 2 — The Preacher's Craft and the Preacher's Source

Kinds of Speaking and Speech Styles

Topics Covered

  • The three kinds of speaking: formal, extemporaneous, impromptu
  • The nine speech styles available to the preacher
  • Choosing the right style for the setting
  • What the debater and the storyteller teach the preacher

Learning Objectives (ABCD method)

  1. By the end of the session, the student will distinguish in writing between the formal, extemporaneous, and impromptu speech, giving one appropriate ministry setting for each.
  2. By the end of the session, the student will name from memory at least seven of the nine speech styles and describe each in one sentence.
  3. By the end of the session, the student will state which kind of speaking is used in most sermons today and explain why, without consulting notes.
  4. By the end of the session, the student will select one speech style and demonstrate it in a two-minute presentation to the class, receiving peer feedback on its effectiveness.

A: audience · B: observable behavior · C: condition · D: degree

I.

Kinds of Speaking

  1. The Formal Speech — an oral presentation that has been completely thought out and written down before its delivery. It is considered formal because the words of its message have been determined beforehand.
  2. The Extemporaneous Speech — an oral presentation in which ideas have been predetermined with no detailed wording. This is used in most sermons today.
  3. The Impromptu Speech — this oral presentation has no specific preparation before its delivery. It can be excellent if the speaker has a rich background of experience on the speaking topic.

Teacher’s Note

Connect back to Session 1: be instant, in season and out of season. The impromptu speech is only as good as the years of preparation behind it.

II.

Speech Styles

  1. The Dramatic Speech — the playing out of a role or roles for the benefit of the audience.
  2. Role-Playing — performed mainly for the benefit of the participating actors rather than for an audience.
  3. Interpretive Reading — a reading of formal material with emotional expression.
  4. Storytelling — speaking which involves the special technique of narrative.
  5. Questions and Answers — this leads listeners to reach the conclusion the speaker has in mind through inviting questions rather than making declarations.
  6. Audio-Visual Aids — a help or reinforcement for many other methods: posters, charts, projected materials.
  7. Discussion — a whole group of people examine a topic or search for consensus in response to a problem.
  8. Debate — each debater takes the attitude that he alone is right and attempts to persuade the listeners to agree. The persuasion skills of a debater are very useful to a preacher in sermon presentation.
  9. Lecture — straight speaker-to-listener speech. It can convey more facts in less time than any other method. It can also be the most boring style.

I.

The Vocabulary of the Sermon

Text

The passage or portion of Scripture with which a sermon deals.

Subject

What the message is about in a definite and clear way.

Title

The name the preacher gives the sermon.

Proposition

A one-sentence summary of the entire message.

Outline

The structural framework of a sermon.

Introduction

The way you begin any sermon, taking it into the body of the message.

Body

The message; how you will get the congregation to the end point.

Content

The meat of the message's ideas and truth.

Conclusion

The final destination of your message; your call to action.

Illustrations

Anything the preacher uses to create a picture in the mind of the listeners.

Context

The total cultural, situational, and literary environment of a passage of Scripture.

Exegesis

The meaning drawn out of a given text.

Exposition

How a text applies to everyday life.

Exhortation

To urge by admonition or imperative the truth found in the Word of God.

Delivery

The way a sermon is presented.

II.

The Three Sermon Models

Textual

A message in which main divisions are derived directly from clear points or divisions in the Bible text itself.

Expository

A message explaining a passage of Scripture and its biblical context.

Topical

A message organized by logical divisions of the subject, rather than divisions of the text.

Teacher’s Note

These three models are worked out in full in Session 20, using the same doctrine — the Holy Spirit — preached three different ways.

III.

The Anatomy of a Sermon

A.

Proposition

Meant to provide clarity and focus. One compelling sentence to summarize your sermon that sets the expectation for what people will be receiving during your message.

B.

Explanation

What does the verse or point mean?

C.

Argument

What are you trying to tell the people about that?

D.

Illustration

What does that look like?

E.

Application

What does your listener need to do?

F.

Transitional Sentence

What is your next point, and how does what you say connect to what you will say next?

  • The first transitional sentence should transition to your first point, not to your message.
  • The last transitional sentence should be getting people ready to answer your call to action.
G.

Conclusion

Call to action. Compel the people to receive what you preached in your message. Bring people to a point of decision.

A: audience · B: observable behavior · C: condition · D: degree

I.

Defining Hermeneutics

Hermeneutics

The science and art of biblical interpretation.

Why hermeneutics is crucial: it guards against misinterpretation, false doctrine, and weak sermons.

Exegesis

Drawing meaning out of the text.

Eisegesis

Reading meaning into the text. This is the danger to be avoided at all costs.

II.

The Pentecostal Hermeneutical Approach

  • Literal interpretation where context allows.
  • Acknowledgment of supernatural elements as historical and normative.
  • The continuing work of the Holy Spirit in illumining and applying Scripture.
  • The importance of balancing the written Word with the living Spirit.
III.

The Four Gaps You Have to Bridge

  1. The Linguistic Gap — the text was not written in your language.
  2. The Cultural Gap — the text was not written in your culture.
  3. The Geographical Gap — the text was not written in your place.
  4. The Historical Gap — the text was not written in your time.

  4. By the end of the session, the student will locate a given word in a Strong's concordance and report its original-language meaning, without assistance.

A: audience · B: observable behavior · C: condition · D: degree

I.

Context

A.

Historical-Cultural Context

Understanding the original audience, author, time period, customs, and events.

B.

Literary Context

Identifying the genre — narrative, poetry, epistle, prophecy — the surrounding verses and chapters, and the book's overall message.

Teacher’s Note

Understand THEN observe, not the other way around.

II.

Observation: What Does It Say?

  • Key words and phrases: repetitions, synonyms, antonyms.
  • Grammar and sentence structure.
  • Connectives such as therefore, but, and for.
  • Figures of speech and rhetorical questions.
  • The who, what, when, where, why, how approach.
III.

Four Methods of Analysis

A.

Contextual Interpretation

Example: understanding the historical and cultural background of the book of Leviticus, recognizing that many of its laws were specific to ancient Israel and their unique covenant with God. This prevents misapplication of those laws to modern-day Christians.

B.

Grammatical and Syntactical Analysis

Example: carefully examining the Greek text of the New Testament, recognizing the nuances of verb tenses, word order, and grammatical constructions to accurately understand the author's intended meaning.

C.

Theological Consistency

Example: interpreting a passage in light of the overall message of the Bible, ensuring that a particular interpretation does not contradict other clear teachings of Scripture — for example, interpreting Revelation while maintaining a balanced view of God's love and justice.

D.

Historical-Critical Method

Example: examining the historical context of the Gospels, considering potential sources, and analyzing the different perspectives of the Gospel writers. This helps to understand the historical Jesus and the early Christian movement.

E.

Basic Exegetical Tools

  • Study Bibles.
  • Concordances, such as Strong's.
  • Bible dictionaries.

  4. The Consequence: The Peace of God, Which Surpasses All Understanding — Philippians 4:7.
  5. Conclusion.
A.

Working the Conclusion

  • Challenge listeners to practice thanksgiving this week, intentionally bringing their worries to God.
  • Invite those who are strangers to this peace into a relationship with Christ, who is its source.
  • Final hope: end with a powerful statement of assurance that the same God who commands us to be anxious for nothing is also the God who provides a peace greater than our anxieties.
  • Prayer: lead a final prayer that applies the truth of the passage, asking God to fill their hearts and minds with His peace.

WEEK 7 — Models and the Preacher's Instrument

The Preacher's Instrument: The Voice

Topics Covered

  • How do I sound? Pitch, speed, fillers, articulation
  • Vocal projection and volume
  • Pace, rhythm and vocal variety
  • The power of the pause
  • Breathing technique and vocal health

Learning Objectives (ABCD method)

  1. By the end of the session, the student will identify the four elements of how do I sound and self-assess his or her own weakest element, in writing.
  2. By the end of the session, the student will list at least six common filler words and demonstrate a two-minute delivery containing none of them.
  3. By the end of the session, the student will explain the strategic use of the pause and demonstrate it at least twice in a practice reading.
  4. By the end of the session, the student will read an assigned passage three times with deliberately varied pace, volume, and pitch, receiving peer feedback on each.

A: audience · B: observable behavior · C: condition · D: degree

I.

How Do I Sound?

  • Pitch and tone.
  • Speed.
  • Fillers — and, um, uh, er, you know, I mean, okay, you see.
  • Articulation: pronunciation and enunciation.

Teacher’s Note

Record students for sixty seconds and play it back. Most preachers have no idea how many fillers they use until they hear themselves.

II.

Developing the Voice

  • Vocal projection and volume: speaking clearly and loudly enough for all to hear without shouting.
  • Pace and rhythm: varying speech speed to emphasize points, create drama, or allow for reflection.
  • Vocal variety in pitch and tone: using different inflections to convey emotion, meaning, and passion.
  • The power of the pause: strategic silence for emphasis, reflection, or to invite the Holy Spirit's work.
  • Breathing techniques and vocal health for sustained speaking.
A.

Activities

  • Vocal exercises: breathing techniques, tongue twisters, reading passages with varied pace and volume.
  • Practice drills: students read a short passage from their sermon outline focusing on one specific vocal skill, such as pace or projection. Instructor and peers provide feedback.

WEEK 8 — Presence, Power and the Journey Ahead

Non-Verbal Power: Body Language and Eye Contact

Topics Covered

  • How do I look? Eyes, posture, movement, gestures
  • The five don'ts and three do's of eye contact
  • Purposeful movement versus nervous pacing
  • Appearance and the microphone

Learning Objectives (ABCD method)

  1. By the end of the session, the student will list the five eye-contact don'ts and the three do's from memory, with at least 80% accuracy.
  2. By the end of the session, the student will distinguish purposeful movement from nervous pacing and demonstrate the difference in a practice segment.
  3. By the end of the session, the student will identify at least two of his or her own habitual gestures and explain why habitual gesture distracts.
  4. By the end of the session, the student will deliver a sermon segment while observing his or her body language, and report at least three specific observations in writing.

A: audience · B: observable behavior · C: condition · D: degree

I.

How Do I Look?

A.

Eyes — The Don'ts

  • Stare at the floor.
  • Look only at friends or those you consider important.
  • Look at those behind you on the platform.
  • Close your eyes.
  • Look at the source of a disturbance.
B.

Eyes — The Do's

  • Make brief eye contact with the entire audience.
  • Look most of the time at the people furthest from you.
  • Look up from your Bible and your notes.
C.

The Rest of the Body

  • Posture — standing confidently, conveying authority and approachability.
  • Movement — purposeful movement rather than nervous pacing.
  • Hands and gestures — natural, purposeful movements that emphasize points rather than distract. Don't form habits.
  • Facial expressions — conveying emotion, sincerity, and connection with the message.
  • Look neat and clean.
  • Don't be afraid of the microphone.
Ecclesiastes 9:8KJV

Let thy garments be always white; and let thy head lack no ointment.

Reference also Proverbs 15:23 and Psalm 34:1. The Holy Spirit leads in how we present ourselves physically.

II.

Activities and Assignment

A.

Mirror Practice

Students stand and practice delivering a sermon segment while observing their body language in a mirror, or with a partner.

B.

Homework

Practice a segment of your sermon, focusing on purposeful body language and making eye contact with various points in the room.

WEEK 8 — Presence, Power and the Journey Ahead

Authenticity, Anointing and Flowing with the Spirit

Topics Covered

  • Authenticity, passion and transparency
  • The anointing defined and its evidences
  • Cultivating and stewarding the anointing
  • The outline as a guide, not a gavel
  • Discernment in the moment

Learning Objectives (ABCD method)

  1. By the end of the session, the student will define the anointing in the Pentecostal context of preaching and name at least four of its evidences.
  2. By the end of the session, the student will list the practices that cultivate the anointing and identify which one he or she most needs to strengthen.
  3. By the end of the session, the student will explain the difference between holding an outline loosely and being unprepared, in writing.
  4. By the end of the session, the student will describe how to discern whether an urge to depart from the outline is the Holy Spirit or personal impulse, giving at least two tests.

A: audience · B: observable behavior · C: condition · D: degree

I.

Authenticity and Connection

  • Authenticity: being genuinely yourself, not trying to imitate others.
  • Passion and conviction: preaching from a deep belief in the message and its power.
  • Sincerity and transparency: being real with the audience, sharing appropriate vulnerability without making it about you.
  • Building rapport: connecting with the audience emotionally and spiritually.
1 Thessalonians 2:8KJV

So being affectionately desirous of you, we were willing to have imparted unto you, not the gospel of God only, but also our own souls, because ye were dear unto us.

Teacher’s Note

Discussion: how do we balance authenticity with the reverence due to the pulpit? What are the dangers of trying to be too cool or too spiritual? Reference 1 Corinthians 2:1-5.

II.

The Anointing

Anointing

The divine enablement, power, and presence of the Holy Spirit upon the preacher to accomplish God's purposes through the sermon.

A.

Evidences of Anointing

  • Conviction.
  • Spiritual understanding.
  • Breakthrough.
  • Healing.
  • Deliverance.
  • Transformation.
B.

Cultivating Anointing

  • Prayer.
  • Fasting.
  • Worship.
  • Obedience.
  • Intimate relationship with God.
  • Living a holy life.
Acts 10:38KJV

How God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Ghost and with power: who went about doing good, and healing all that were oppressed of the devil; for God was with him.

Reference also Luke 4:18-19 and 1 John 2:20, 27.

III.

Flowing with the Spirit

  • The outline as a guide, not a gavel: hold the outline loosely, allowing the Holy Spirit to adjust, expand, or even shorten.
  • Spontaneous utterances: words of knowledge, prophetic words, spontaneous prayer, or worship moments during the sermon.
  • Discernment in the moment: knowing when to stick to the plan and when to yield to the Spirit's promptings.
  • Creating space for the Spirit: allowing moments of silence, inviting congregational participation as appropriate.
  • The dangers of being rigid or unprepared — and the dangers of being completely unscripted without true spiritual leading.
1 Corinthians 14:32-33KJV

And the spirits of the prophets are subject to the prophets. [33] For God is not the author of confusion, but of peace, as in all churches of the saints.

Reference also John 3:8 — the Spirit blows where He wills.

A.

Homework

Practice delivering your full sermon, but intentionally pause at a few points and imagine the Holy Spirit prompting you to say something additional or pray for someone. How would you integrate that?

WEEK 8 — Presence, Power and the Journey Ahead

Delivery, Feedback and the Journey Ahead

Topics Covered

  • Overcoming nervousness, distraction and opposition
  • The preacher's character as the foundation
  • Practice sermonette deliveries
  • Giving and receiving constructive feedback
  • Lifelong learning and the final charge

Learning Objectives (ABCD method)

  1. By the end of the session, the student will name at least three strategies for managing nervousness and identify which he or she will use.
  2. By the end of the session, the student will deliver a five to seven minute segment of a prepared sermon, demonstrating biblical soundness, clear structure, and reliance on the Holy Spirit.
  3. By the end of the session, the student will give constructive feedback to a peer that is specific, actionable, and balanced, using the class rubric.
  4. By the end of the session, the student will write a personal action plan naming two to three specific steps to be taken in the next six to twelve months to continue developing as a preacher.

A: audience · B: observable behavior · C: condition · D: degree

I.

Overcoming Obstacles and Maintaining Character

  • Dealing with nervousness and the fear of man: prayer, deep breaths, focus on God, preparation.
  • Distractions: how to handle interruptions, unengaged audience members, or personal mental distractions.
  • Spiritual warfare in the pulpit: recognizing and resisting spiritual opposition to the message.
  • The preacher's character: integrity, humility, purity, love, and spiritual disciplines as foundational for sustained anointing and ministry effectiveness.
  • The Holy Spirit works through character, not just giftedness.

Reference 1 Timothy 3:1-7, Titus 1:5-9, and Hebrews 12:1-2.

Teacher’s Note

Open forum: students share their biggest fears or challenges regarding delivery, and the class brainstorms solutions. Then ask: why is a preacher's personal walk with God even more important than their preaching skill?

II.

Practice Sermonette Deliveries

  • Application of all learned principles: biblical soundness, structure, communication technique, and reliance on the Holy Spirit.
  • Confidence building: overcoming the fear of public speaking through practice.
  • Live experience: simulating a real preaching moment.
A.

Presentation Requirement

Each student delivers a five to seven minute segment of the full sermon — for example, the introduction and first main point — using at least four of the five sermon elements, including an illustration or an argument.

Immediately after delivery, each student notes their own observations, highlighting strengths and areas for improvement, especially regarding sensing the Holy Spirit's presence. Reference Philippians 4:13 and 1 Timothy 4:13-16.

III.

Feedback and Growth

A.

Principles of Constructive Feedback

  • Specific and actionable.
  • Balanced — strengths first.
  • Encouraging.
  • Focused on the message and delivery, not the person.
B.

Receiving Feedback

  • Humility.
  • Open-mindedness.
  • Discerning the Holy Spirit's voice through others.
Proverbs 27:17KJV

Iron sharpeneth iron; so a man sharpeneth the countenance of his friend.

C.

The Journey Ahead

  • Continual Bible study: a preacher's well never runs dry if they stay in the Word.
  • Ongoing prayer and spiritual disciplines: the fuel for ministry.
  • Seeking mentorship and accountability: the importance of wise counsel and spiritual covering.
  • Personal character development: preaching flows from who you are in Christ.
  • The Spirit-led journey: trusting God for open doors, new opportunities, and fresh anointing.

Teacher’s Note

Final charge: Go and preach the Gospel with power and purpose. Close with a commissioning prayer — instructor and students praying over one another for God's anointing, protection, and fruitfulness. Homework: keep preaching.
